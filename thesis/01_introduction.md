# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Problem Statement and Relevance of Digital Behaviour Change Interventions

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) — cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, chronic respiratory disease and many cancers — account for roughly three quarters of global mortality, and a substantial share of their burden is attributable to a small set of modifiable behaviours: physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, insufficient or irregular sleep, tobacco use and harmful alcohol consumption. Physical inactivity alone illustrates the scale of the problem. Despite unambiguous evidence for the dose–response relationship between moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and all-cause mortality, which underpins the World Health Organization's recommendation of 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week (Bull et al., 2020), an estimated 31% of adults worldwide — approximately 1.8 billion people — were insufficiently active in 2022, and the trend has been worsening rather than improving (Strain et al., 2024). The direct health-care costs of inactivity have been estimated in the tens of billions of international dollars per year, before productivity losses are considered (Ding et al., 2016).

The behavioural nature of these risk factors implies that the decisive therapeutic event does not occur in the clinic. It occurs in the hundreds of ordinary moments in a person's week in which a choice between a healthier and a less healthy option is made — taking the stairs, going to bed rather than continuing to scroll, preparing a meal rather than ordering one. Traditional face-to-face interventions are poorly matched to this structure: they are delivered episodically, at times dictated by the provider, and they rely on the person to transfer insight from the consultation room into everyday life. The well-documented *intention–behaviour gap* shows how fragile this transfer is. In a meta-analysis applying the action control framework to physical activity, Rhodes and de Bruijn (2013) found that 48% of people who formed an intention to be active failed to act on it. Sheeran and Webb (2016) attribute the gap to failures in initiating action, in maintaining action against competing goals, and in disengaging from unhelpful routines — precisely the processes that unfold *between* consultations.

Smartphones and wearable sensors appeared to offer the missing link. They are carried continuously, they can deliver support in situ, and they generate dense streams of data about movement, physiology, sleep, location and schedule. Consequently, digital behaviour change interventions (DBCIs) have proliferated, and several hundred thousand health-related applications are available in commercial app stores. Their promise rests on three capabilities that are not available to conventional programmes: *ubiquity* (support can be provided at any moment), *sensing* (the system can observe behaviour and context without relying exclusively on self-report), and *adaptivity* (the content and timing of support can be continuously tailored to the individual).

The empirical record, however, is sobering. Meta-analyses of mHealth interventions for physical activity report small-to-moderate short-term effects that diminish markedly over longer follow-up periods (Mönninghoff et al., 2021). Engagement — a necessary precondition for any digitally mediated effect — collapses rapidly: in a panel-based analysis of mental health applications, Baumel et al. (2019) observed a median 15-day retention of 3.9% and a 30-day retention of 3.3%. Eysenbach (2005) described this pattern as the "law of attrition" more than a decade before smartphone apps became the dominant delivery channel, and it has proven remarkably persistent. Notifications, the primary mechanism through which DBCIs try to reach users at the right moment, are frequently dismissed or ignored, and excessive or ill-timed prompting produces habituation and alert fatigue, which further erode the channel through which future support must flow.

The relevance of the problem addressed in this thesis therefore lies at the intersection of two observations. First, the health and economic stakes of everyday behaviour are very large, and digital technology is, in principle, the only scalable means of providing support at the temporal resolution at which behaviour occurs. Second, the current generation of DBCIs systematically fails to exploit this potential. The question is *why*. Section 1.2 argues that a substantial part of the answer is that most systems treat the user as a static target to be informed, reminded and motivated, rather than as a dynamic system whose capacity to respond to support changes from hour to hour.

## 1.2 The Fundamental Weakness of Existing mHealth Systems: Ignoring Receptivity

The dominant design pattern of commercial health applications can be summarised as *goal → plan → reminder → feedback*. The user (or the system) sets a goal such as 10,000 steps per day or three workouts per week; the system translates the goal into a plan; reminders are issued at fixed or user-selected times; and progress is fed back via dashboards, streaks and badges. Many of these elements correspond to evidence-based behaviour change techniques (BCTs) — goal setting, action planning, prompts and cues, self-monitoring and feedback on behaviour (Michie et al., 2013) — and their inclusion is not the problem. The problem is the implicit model of the person on which they rely.

That implicit model is *stationary*: it assumes that the same prompt, delivered with the same content, is equally likely to be noticed, processed and acted upon at 08:00 on a Monday after a night of poor sleep and a full calendar as at 18:00 on a relaxed Saturday. Under this assumption, failure to act is attributed either to insufficient motivation, which leads to more persuasive messaging, or to forgetting, which leads to more reminders. Neither response engages with the actual reason why, on a particular day, the planned behaviour did not happen.

Contemporary behavioural science provides a richer account. The COM-B model (Michie et al., 2011) posits that behaviour occurs only when *capability*, *opportunity* and *motivation* are jointly sufficient. Each of these components is partly trait-like — a person's general fitness, their typical working hours, their long-term values — but each also has a substantial *state* component that fluctuates on short time scales. Physical capability is reduced after poor sleep or illness; psychological capability (attention, executive control) is reduced under acute stress and cognitive load (Hofmann et al., 2012); physical opportunity disappears when meetings are stacked back to back; automatic motivation varies with affect and fatigue. The barriers that prevent behaviour are therefore not fixed attributes of the person but *time-varying latent states*.

Receptivity is the umbrella concept under which these fluctuations become relevant for intervention design. In the foundational framework for just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs), Nahum-Shani et al. (2018) define receptivity as the person's ability to receive, process and use the support provided, and they distinguish it from *states of vulnerability* (moments of elevated risk) and *states of opportunity* (moments in which positive change is particularly feasible). Empirical work in ubiquitous computing has confirmed that receptivity is measurable and predictable from context: the likelihood that a person engages with a health prompt depends on time of day, day of week, device state, physical activity, location and personal traits (Künzler et al., 2019; Mehrotra et al., 2016), and machine-learned receptivity models can significantly increase the rate of timely engagement compared with randomly timed prompts (Mishra et al., 2021). Micro-randomised trials have shown that the proximal effect of identical prompts differs substantially by context and decays over time in the study (Klasnja et al., 2019; Bidargaddi et al., 2018).

Despite this evidence, receptivity is rarely represented as a first-class quantity in deployed systems. Where it is considered at all, it is typically handled in one of three insufficient ways:

1. **As a timing filter only.** The system predicts *whether* to send a prompt (e.g. not while driving, not during a meeting), but the *content and size* of the requested behaviour remain unchanged. A user who is reachable but exhausted is still asked for a 30-minute run.
2. **As a fixed user preference.** Users select preferred notification windows during onboarding. This captures stable routines but not day-to-day deviations, which are precisely the moments in which plans fail.
3. **As an unmodelled residual.** Data-driven optimisers (e.g. bandit algorithms maximising engagement) implicitly absorb receptivity into their reward signal, but without an interpretable state representation. They cannot explain *why* a given day is difficult, cannot transfer this knowledge across intervention types, and cannot distinguish between "this action is ineffective for this person" and "this action was infeasible today".

The last point deserves emphasis, because it marks the conceptual core of this thesis. When a proposed action is not executed, the non-execution is *evidence* — but evidence about what? It may indicate low benefit perception (a motivational barrier), a structural lack of time (an opportunity barrier), physical depletion (a capability barrier), or simply that the prompt was never seen (low receptivity). A system that does not model these alternatives as competing explanations will conflate them, and will therefore learn the wrong lesson: it may conclude that walking is not a suitable behaviour for a user who in fact simply had no free slot between 09:00 and 17:00 on that day. Conversely, a system that maintains an explicit probabilistic model of barriers can treat each executed or missed action as a likelihood term that updates its beliefs about the person's current and typical states — a process of *behaviour understanding* rather than mere behaviour tracking.

A second, closely related weakness concerns the *granularity of the requested behaviour*. Most systems operate with a fixed target and treat partial achievement as failure. Yet behavioural theory and practice converge on the observation that, on difficult days, a much smaller version of the behaviour — two minutes of stretching instead of a 30-minute workout, one glass of water instead of a full meal plan — preserves the behavioural chain, protects self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977) and contributes to habit formation through context-dependent repetition (Lally et al., 2010; Wood & Neal, 2007). Fogg (2009, 2019) has formalised this intuition by arguing that ability and motivation trade off against each other: when motivation or ability is low, the behaviour must be made easier for a prompt to succeed. What is lacking is a *quantitative decision rule* that determines, for a given person at a given moment, how small the step must be.

In summary, existing mHealth systems exhibit three linked deficits: (i) they do not represent the person's momentary barriers as latent, time-varying states; (ii) they do not interpret execution outcomes as evidence about those states; and (iii) they do not adapt the *size* of the requested behaviour to the probability that it will actually be performed. This thesis addresses all three.

## 1.3 Objective and Core Scientific Hypothesis

The objective of this thesis is to design, formalise and evaluate a *Behaviour Understanding Engine* (BUE): a decision system for personalised health promotion that (a) senses the barriers a person faces at a given moment, (b) selects the smallest action that is still likely to be performed while maximising its health value, and (c) continuously learns a person-specific model from the outcomes of its own proposals until the target behaviour becomes self-sustaining.

### 1.3.1 The Expected Health Gain as Decision Criterion

The engine's decision criterion follows from a simple decomposition. The health value of proposing an action $a$ at decision point $t$ is realised only if the action is actually performed. Let $Y_t \in \{0,1\}$ indicate execution and let $B_t(a)$ denote the (uncertain) health benefit that accrues if $a$ is executed. Assuming that a non-executed proposal yields no direct benefit, the expected value of proposing $a$ is

$$
\mathrm{EHG}_t(a) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[B_t(a)\,Y_t \mid a, \mathcal{H}_t\right]
\;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[B_t(a) \mid Y_t = 1, \mathcal{H}_t\right]}_{\text{expected benefit}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{P\!\left(Y_t = 1 \mid a, \mathcal{H}_t\right)}_{\text{probability of action}},
\tag{1.1}
$$

where $\mathcal{H}_t$ is the history of observations available to the engine. The policy selects

$$
a_t^\ast \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \; \mathrm{EHG}_t(a).
\tag{1.2}
$$

Equation (1.1) is deliberately simple, but it makes the central trade-off explicit. Ambitious actions have high conditional benefit but low execution probability; trivial actions are almost certainly executed but contribute little. Conventional systems implicitly optimise only the first factor, setting targets according to guidelines while treating execution probability as constant. Engagement-optimising systems implicitly optimise only the second, maximising responses irrespective of health value. The EHG criterion requires both to be modelled jointly, and it is the *probability factor* that carries the dependence on the person's momentary state.

The novelty of the present approach lies in how this probability is constructed. Rather than estimating $P(Y_t = 1 \mid a, \mathcal{H}_t)$ as a black-box function of raw context, the engine factorises it through two latent quantities: the momentary *receptivity* $\rho_t$, which governs whether a prompt is attended to at all, and a vector of *barrier intensities* $\mathbf{b}_t \in [0,1]^K$, which governs whether an attended prompt is converted into behaviour. Marginalising over the posterior belief about these latent states yields

$$
P\!\left(Y_t = 1 \mid a, \mathcal{H}_t\right) \;=\; \int \rho_t \cdot \pi_a(\mathbf{b}_t;\boldsymbol{\theta})\; p\!\left(\rho_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{H}_t\right)\, d\rho_t\, d\mathbf{b}_t\, d\boldsymbol{\theta},
\tag{1.3}
$$

where $\pi_a(\mathbf{b};\boldsymbol{\theta}) = P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1, a, \mathbf{b}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the conditional execution probability given engagement, parameterised by person-level parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that encode, for example, how strongly a given barrier suppresses a given class of action. The posterior $p(\rho_t, \mathbf{b}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{H}_t)$ is maintained by a Bayesian filter that fuses heterogeneous evidence — calendar density, heart-rate variability and sleep metrics from wearables, historical success rates and, crucially, the observed outcomes $R_{t'}$ and $Y_{t'}$ of earlier proposals. The full specification of $B_t(a)$, $\pi_a$ and the filter is developed in Chapters 3 and 4.

### 1.3.2 Hypotheses

The central claim of the thesis is formulated as one core hypothesis and three subsidiary hypotheses.

> **Core hypothesis (H0).** A decision policy that explicitly models momentary receptivity and latent barriers, and that selects actions by maximising the Expected Health Gain (Eq. 1.1), achieves a higher cumulative realised health benefit than policies that either ignore momentary state or optimise benefit and execution probability separately.

> **H1 (Barrier inference).** Latent barrier states can be inferred from passively sensed evidence (calendar, wearable physiology, contextual features) and from execution outcomes with sufficient accuracy to improve the prediction of execution relative to a context-only model without latent structure.

> **H2 (Smallest Step Strategy).** Scaling the requested behaviour down along a graded dose ladder when execution probability is low increases cumulative realised benefit and the long-run execution rate relative to fixed-dose prompting, because it preserves behavioural repetition and avoids the self-efficacy costs of repeated failure.

> **H3 (Interpretable learning).** Treating non-execution as evidence that is apportioned among competing explanations (low receptivity versus specific barriers) leads to faster and more accurate learning of person-specific parameters than treating non-execution as uniform negative reward.

Hypothesis H0 is a statement about a policy class and is evaluated primarily in simulation (Chapter 5), where ground truth is available. H1–H3 decompose H0 into mechanisms that can be tested in isolation through ablations and, prospectively, through a micro-randomised trial whose design is specified in Section 5.7.

### 1.3.3 Research Questions

The hypotheses translate into the following research questions, which structure the main part of the thesis:

- **RQ1.** How can the expected benefit and the execution probability of a health action be formally defined and decomposed such that their dependence on latent barriers and receptivity becomes explicit? *(Chapter 3)*
- **RQ2.** How can a Bayesian barrier model be constructed that fuses calendar, wearable and behavioural evidence via prior–posterior updates into a coherent estimate of the user's current state? *(Chapter 4, Sections 4.1–4.5)*
- **RQ3.** By what decision rule should an intervention be scaled down when its execution probability is insufficient, and how should it be scaled up again as habits consolidate? *(Sections 4.6–4.8)*
- **RQ4.** Under which conditions does a receptivity-driven, EHG-maximising engine outperform established baselines, and how can this be validated empirically? *(Chapter 5)*

## 1.4 Contributions

This thesis makes four contributions.

1. **A formal decision-theoretic framework.** The Expected Health Gain criterion is developed from a heuristic product into a fully specified objective in which the benefit term includes both immediate physiological value and the contribution of an executed action to habit strength and self-efficacy, and in which the probability term is factorised into receptivity and barrier-conditioned execution. This makes the "smallest effective step" a derived quantity rather than a design heuristic.
2. **A Bayesian barrier model grounded in COM-B.** The thesis specifies a dynamic latent-state model whose barrier dimensions map onto the components of the COM-B model, whose observation models link these dimensions to concrete data streams, and whose updates treat execution and non-execution outcomes as informative evidence. To the author's knowledge, the explicit integration of behaviour-theoretic barrier constructs, multi-source passive sensing and outcome-driven belief updating within one generative model has not previously been described for JITAIs.
3. **The Smallest Step Strategy.** An algorithm is derived that scales a BCT-annotated intervention along a dose ladder to the point at which the marginal loss in benefit is balanced by the marginal gain in execution probability, subject to a floor on execution probability that protects self-efficacy, together with a progression rule for re-escalation as habit strength increases.
4. **An evaluation methodology and in-silico evidence.** The thesis provides a generative simulation environment with heterogeneous synthetic personas, baseline policies and outcome metrics, and uses it to evaluate the engine. Beyond confirming the design, the evaluation yields two findings that redirect it. First, information about free time is the most valuable input, and a short daily conversation can substitute for most passive sensing. Second, unrestricted switching between health domains undermines habit formation. The evaluation is complemented by the design of a micro-randomised trial for empirical validation.

## 1.5 Structure of the Thesis

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. **Chapter 2** reviews the theoretical and empirical foundations on which the engine is built. It covers models of health behaviour change, the COM-B model and the Behaviour Change Wheel, the BCT Taxonomy, theories of minimal behaviours and habit formation, the engagement problem in digital interventions, the JITAI framework, empirical research on receptivity, micro-randomised trials, sequential decision-making algorithms used in mHealth, Bayesian latent-state models, and passive sensing. The chapter closes with a synthesis that locates the research gap. **Chapter 3** develops the architecture of the engine, including the formal EHG framework, the human model with its COM-B barrier ontology, the graded action space and the data sources. **Chapter 4** specifies the algorithms: the generative barrier model, prior specification and hierarchical pooling, observation models, posterior inference, the learning of execution probabilities, the Smallest Step Strategy, exploration via Thompson sampling, and habit-dependent progression. **Chapter 5** evaluates the engine in simulation against baseline policies across a set of scenarios and specifies a micro-randomised trial for empirical validation. **Chapter 6** discusses implications, limitations and ethical considerations, outlines transfer to other domains such as medication adherence, mental health and chronic disease self-management, and proposes extensions.
